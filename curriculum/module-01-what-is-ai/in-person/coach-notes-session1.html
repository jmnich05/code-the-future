10:15–10:35🎯 The Bad AI Challenge

The why (your study notes)
AI is a prediction machine — it was trained on millions of examples and predicts the most likely response to your words. A vague prompt like "make me a game" matches everything it has ever seen, so it predicts the average of all games — which is boring by definition. Specific details narrow the pattern space, so the output snaps toward YOUR idea. That's the whole lesson: generic in, generic out; specific in, specific out.
Say it like this
"AI doesn't read your mind. It reads your words. If your words could mean a thousand things, you get the most boring one of the thousand."
Round 1 · "Make me a game" — discussion moves
  • "Is this what you imagined? What did YOU picture in your head?" — every kid pictured something different; the AI had to guess. That's the aha.
  • "What did we forget to tell it?" — let them build the checklist out loud (characters, setting, goal, rules, mood). Board them.
  • If the AI happens to return something decent: "Okay — but is it YOURS? Could it have given this exact thing to any kid in any city?"
Round 2 · the group rewrite
  • Take suggestions ONE detail at a time and re-run when the prompt has ~5. Kids watching the output improve in real time is the lesson working.
  • "Which single detail changed the result the most?" — usually the character or the setting. Point out: that's debugging — change one thing, watch what happens.
Round 3 · their own prompts
  • The five-detail rule is the guardrail. Kenya circulates with her reflective questions; you demo the best 2–3 on screen.
  • Praise the revision, not the result: "I love that you added the mood — hear how the whole thing changed?"
  • "What's one MORE detail that would make it even more yours?" — plants the idea that prompts are never done, just improving. That's iteration — how real builders work.

10:35–10:50🧠 Your mini-lecture — the 5 points with their "why"

1 · AI is a tool, not a brain

The why: it has no goals, no feelings, no understanding — it computes "what usually comes next" from patterns in its training examples. It can SOUND thoughtful because it learned from thoughtful writing.

Ask: "When it wrote that game idea, was it excited about it?" (No — YOU were. The excitement in the room is all human.)

2 · Prompting is communication

The why: the model can only work with what's in the prompt (plus its training). Missing details get filled in with the most statistically common thing — which is why unnamed characters come out generic.

Ask: "Have you ever given directions to someone and they did it 'wrong' — but really they did exactly what you SAID?" Same thing.

3 · Coding and AI both require precision

The why: computers are perfectly literal. In code, one wrong step in a sequence breaks everything — and fixing it (debugging) is a normal, daily part of the job, not a failure. Prompt revision IS debugging: change one thing, run it, observe.

Ask: "Tell a robot to make a peanut butter sandwich — what's step one?" (Someone says "put on the peanut butter" — "On WHAT? The robot doesn't know bread exists yet!")

4 · AI can be helpful, but not always correct

The why: it predicts plausible answers, not true ones — when it doesn't know, it still generates something confident-sounding. And it can only reflect the examples it learned from, so gaps and biases in those examples show up in its answers. That's why the human checks.

Ask: "If AI sounds 100% sure, does that mean it's right?" (Nope — confidence and correctness are different things. YOU are the fact-checker.)

5 · Creators lead the technology

The why: the idea, the taste, the judgment calls, the "that's not quite it, try again" — all human. AI multiplies your idea; it can't originate your idea. This is the camp's whole thesis: code does the loop, AI does the judgment inside the loop, humans handle the exceptions — and kids handle the imagination.

Ask: "Whose game will it be when you're done — yours or the computer's?"

Appendix📡 Live Room answer key — the WHY behind every question

If you run the game show (host screen → Next/Reveal), these are the current questions, the answers, the deeper reason, and a follow-up to ask before you hit Reveal.

🤔 Warm-up · "AI is most like…"opinion

No wrong answer — it's a thermometer of the room. The camp's answers are 📚 pattern learner and 🐶 puppy you train: both capture "learns from examples." The one to gently retire is 🔮 magic: magic can't be inspected, improved, or debugged — but a pattern machine can, and that's why kids can BUILD with it.

Ask a defender of each option "why?" before the reveal — then: "By August, watch — most of you will vote puppy."

🐶 Round 1 · Photo filter that gives you dog earsAI

Why: the filter learned where eyes, noses, and heads are from millions of face photos — that's how the ears stick to YOU while you move. It's live pattern-spotting (face landmarks), not a sticker on a picture.

Ask: "How does it know where your ears go when you turn your head?" — kids who say "it just knows" are describing the trained pattern without realizing it.

Misconception to catch: "it's just a camera trick." A plain camera doesn't know a face from a shoe — the KNOWING is the AI.

🚪 Round 2 · Automatic doors at the storeNOT AI

Why: one sensor, one fixed rule — something moves close, door opens. It never learns, never improves, treats everyone identically forever.

Ask: "Does the door get BETTER at opening the more you visit?" (No → no learning → not AI.) That question — "does it learn?" — is the master key for every round.

🤖 Round 3 · Robot that learns to walk by falling downAI

Why: every fall is a training example. It tries, wobbles, adjusts, tries again — trial-and-error learning (the fancy word is reinforcement learning). Its teacher is its own mistakes.

Ask: "Where did its examples come from if nobody gave it any?" (Its own falls!) Then the connection kids love: "How did YOU learn to ride a bike?" Falling isn't failure — it's data.

🥤 Round 4 · Vending machine dropping your snackNOT AI

Why: press B4 → get chips. A fixed map from buttons to snacks, zero learning, zero guessing. It would hand a robber the same chips.

Ask: "Could this machine ever surprise you?" — fixed-rule machines can't surprise; learning machines can. (Nice callback to the Bad AI Challenge: the surprise is exactly what prediction adds.)

🚗 Round 5 · A car that drives itselfAI

Why: trained on millions of miles of driving to recognize roads, people, stop signs — and it keeps learning from new miles.

Ask: "If it learned from millions of miles, why do they still keep a human ready to grab the wheel?" — because AI handles the pattern, humans handle the exceptions (the weird stuff it's never seen). That's the camp's core work rule hiding in a car.

🎲 Round 6 · Rolling dice in a board game appNOT AI

Why: random isn't smart — picking a random number takes no learning at all. Sneaky-good final round: random FEELS unpredictable like AI, but it's the opposite of a pattern.

Ask: "The dice surprise you every time — so why isn't THAT learning?" (Because it never gets better. Surprise without improvement is just chance.)

♟️ Mythbusters 1 · "An AI has beaten the world champion at chess"TRUE

Why: true since 1997 — Deep Blue beat world champion Garry Kasparov, before smartphones existed. It studied millions of games; pattern power at scale beats even a champion.

Ask AFTER the reveal: "So is that computer smarter than the champion at EVERYTHING?" — no! It can't tie a shoe or tell a joke. AI is a superhero at ONE narrow thing at a time. That nuance is the real lesson.

😴 Mythbusters 2 · "AI gets smarter all by itself while you sleep"MYTH

Why: AI only improves when PEOPLE train it — feeding it new examples, correcting it, rebuilding it. No builders, no learning. (Kids often imagine AI as a creature that grows on its own — this is the de-spooking question.)

Ask: "So who decides what an AI gets better at?" — people. Builders. "That's the job you're auditioning for this summer."

🧒 Mythbusters 3 · "AI is only for grown-up engineers"MYTH

Why: the biggest myth in the room — and your segue. They already commanded a real AI this morning in the Bad AI Challenge, and in about one minute they pick the real app they'll build with one.

Ask: "Who in this room has already bossed an AI around today?" (Every hand goes up.) "Then I guess the myth is busted." → hit Next, straight into the Big Build pick on that energy.

Grab bag🧰 Metaphor bank — AI in 9-to-12-year-old

🍎 The Apple trick: name the familiar thing FIRST, then map it. "You know how ___? AI is like that, except ___." Let the kid finish the mapping — "so what would the treats be for a computer?" — and the metaphor becomes theirs.
🐶 What AI is · learning from examples

Puppy training. You don't hand a puppy a rulebook — you show it, reward it, repeat. AI learns tricks the same way: examples and "good job" signals, thousands of times a second.

How YOU learned "dog." Nobody gave you a dog definition when you were two. You saw a bunch of dogs, someone said "dog," and your brain built the pattern. AI does exactly that — with a million dogs.

Sorting laundry. After enough loads you just KNOW a sock from a shirt without thinking. That "just knowing" feeling is what a trained pattern feels like.

The flashcard kid. Imagine a kid who studied a billion flashcards but never went outside. Amazing at answering — but everything it "knows" came from the cards. That's AI: brilliant, and card-limited.

Free throws. Shoot 10,000 free throws and your arm "learns" the shot — no rule sheet, just reps and adjustment. Training an AI is reps and adjustment.

🔮 Prediction · how it answers

The world's best autocomplete. Your phone keyboard guesses your next word from YOUR texts. AI is that — except it read the whole library first.

Finish the lyric. "Twinkle, twinkle, little ___" — your brain yelled STAR before I finished. AI does that with everything, one word at a time.

The weather forecaster. Nobody can SEE tomorrow — forecasters predict from patterns in a zillion past days. AI never "knows"; it forecasts words.

Guess the next domino. It doesn't plan the whole sentence — it places one word, looks at the line, places the next.

🧠 Neural networks · what's inside

A stadium of tiny voters. Millions of tiny switches each get a say — "cat? not cat?" — and the votes add up to an answer. No single switch is smart; the CROWD is.

A Plinko board. Drop a question in the top, it bounces through millions of pegs, an answer falls out the bottom. Training = nudging pegs so better answers fall out.

Dimmer switches, not light switches. Real neurons and fake ones both work in "a little more, a little less" — not just ON/OFF. Learning is a million tiny nudges.

🎯 Attention · what matters in a sentence

The highlighter. Before answering, AI runs a highlighter over your words and presses harder on the ones that matter most.

Your name at recess. A hundred kids yelling, and you still hear YOUR name across the playground. That filter — ignore almost everything, lock onto what matters — is attention.

👁️ AI vision

The Where's Waldo champion. Look at enough pages and you start spotting the stripes instantly. AI looked at millions of pages.

Connect-the-dots in reverse. You see the picture and find the dots; AI sees a zillion dots (pixels) and finds the picture.

⚙️ Machine vs. AI · fixed rules vs. learning

Vending machine vs. lemonade-stand kid. The machine gives the same chips to everyone forever. The kid notices "hot day = make double" — noticing and adjusting is the AI part.

Recipe vs. chef. A recipe follows the exact same steps every time. A chef tastes and adjusts. Old-school code is the recipe; AI is closer to the chef.

Player piano vs. musician. Both make music. One replays holes punched in a roll; the other learned from every song it ever heard.

The master question: "Does it get BETTER with practice?" No → machine. Yes → AI. Works on literally everything in their house.

😅 Mistakes · confidently wrong

The friend who never says "I don't know." Fun at trivia night, usually right, ALWAYS sure — even when wrong. That's why you double-check.

Autocorrect. Kids already know this one in their bones: it changes your word to the wrong word with total confidence. Same machinery, same lesson.

Dreaming. A dream feels completely real while you're in it. When AI makes something up, it isn't lying — it's "dreaming" an answer that fits the pattern. Your job: wake it up and check.

⚖️ Bias · it only knows what it was shown

The golden-retriever problem. If every dog you ever saw was a golden retriever, you'd swear ALL dogs are golden. AI only "saw" what its builders showed it — gaps in the photos become gaps in the brain.

The picky eater food critic. Judges every food in the world — but has only ever tasted ten things.

💬 Prompting · why details matter

Ordering pizza. "Bring me food" gets you SOMETHING. "Large pepperoni, thin crust, no olives" gets you dinner. AI delivers exactly what you ordered, not what you meant.

The literal genie. Grants exactly the words of the wish. Wish sloppy, get sloppy. Great prompting is careful wishing.

Directions for a robot butler. "Make a sandwich" — it stares at you. It doesn't know bread exists until you say so.

🛠️ Ownership · the camp's big one

Power drill vs. screwdriver. The drill is a hundred times faster — but it has NO idea where the hole goes. That's you. It never stops being you.

E-bike for your brain. You still steer, you still pedal — it just makes every push go further.

Sled dogs. Strong, fast, tireless — and the musher picks the destination. Be the musher.

The orchestra. AI is a hundred instruments that can play anything. The kid is the conductor — no conductor, no song.

The parrot with a library card. It can repeat anything from every book ever written — beautifully. But it's never MEANT a single word. The meaning is your job.

🔁 Bonus · loops & helpers (plants seeds for later)

The Roomba rule. The robot does the boring loop, all day, happily. But when it eats a sock — a human handles it. Code does the loop, AI does the judgment, humans handle the exceptions.

A thousand helpers who each read one book. Ask a question and they all whisper what they found at once. That's how AI "read everything" — teamwork at silly speed.
